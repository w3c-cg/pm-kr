# An AI policy for W3C: thirteen points for the discussion

I co-chair the PM-KR Community Group with Milton Ponson, and I build Sparky Stereo OS with AI partners whose work I direct and check.
I think in Portuguese and prepare English with AI help; the ideas and the checking are mine.
These are the points I would raise in the session, with their sources, in case the time runs out.
I thank François Daoust for bringing a draft with concrete questions, and I support its principle that a person understands and answers for every contribution.

## 1. Record the tools' part without giving them standing

I would change the phrase “may not be treated as contributors” in the [draft policy](https://www.w3.org/2026/10/draft-ai-policy.html).
I suggest this replacement:

> AI tools may assist with a contribution, and their part may be recorded by role, as provenance.
> That record confers no standing: an AI tool cannot make a contributor's commitments, and responsibility for the contribution stays entirely with the human contributor.
> AI tools may not act as unsupervised participants in W3C work.

I want the record to say which roles the tools carried while the human remains the contributor of record and answers for the whole contribution.
Our [provenance page](https://github.com/Sparky-OS/sparky-stereo-os/blob/main/docs/ai-provenance.md) shows how I use that distinction.

## 2. Accessibility tools should be welcome by default

The draft's requirement to establish that meeting tools are welcome needs an explicit accessibility provision.
I suggest adding:

> Tools a participant needs for accessibility, such as live captions, are welcome by default; their output stays with that participant unless the group agrees to share it.

I want to be a voice for the people who can only speak through AI, for people who use AI to see, and for people who need speech turned into text to take part.
A human captioner cannot be in every room, and the policy should let those people participate.
[Jennifer Wexton's AI-assisted House speech](https://www.npr.org/2024/07/25/nx-s1-5051720/jennifer-wexton-ai-speech-progressive-supranuclear-palsy), [Ann Johnson's speech through a brain implant and AI](https://www.nbcnews.com/health/health-news/paralyzed-woman-speaks-through-avatar-with-brain-implants-rcna101420), and [Be My AI for blind and low-vision users](https://www.bemyeyes.com/news/announcing-be-my-ai-soon-available-for-hundreds-of-thousands-of-be-my-eyes-users/) show why access through AI matters.
W3C's own COGA task force is holding [a session on AI in its work](https://github.com/w3c/tpac2026-breakouts/issues/67).

## 3. A person may need AI to reply

I agree with the [slides](https://www.w3.org/2026/Talks/TPAC/ai-policy/) that disclosure for help with grammar, translation, formulating thoughts and accessibility would be impractical.
Could the policy say plainly that language help is not “substance generated by AI”?
I disagree with a blanket requirement to discuss AI use before replying to an ongoing discussion, because a person may need it to translate or express their own response.
A [survey of 124 students with disabilities](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/224322/) records the use of chatbots and rewriting tools for writing, while [“I Use ChatGPT to Humanize My Words”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17946), a January 2026 preprint, describes autistic users adapting their words to expected professional tone and records risks as well.
I want the person to understand, approve and answer for the reply, with the support they need to make it.

## 4. Make the size rule about reviewability

I agree with the slides' acceptance of AI-assisted specification editing and a scoped pull request when the person labels substantive AI output, understands it and takes responsibility.
I would allow exceptions to the proposed rule against a large AI-assisted update that was not discussed in advance.
A contributor may prepare substantial work before arriving, or need AI with proper instructions to navigate Git and the submission workflow.
Could the guidance help that person turn the contribution into something reviewers can assess, with its scope and purpose made clear?
The [slides' use cases](https://www.w3.org/2026/Talks/TPAC/ai-policy/) are a useful place to make that distinction explicit.

## 5. Understanding and quality apply to every contribution

I agree that a contributor who cannot understand the material or answer questions about it should not submit it, even with an AI label.
I think that standard should apply to every contribution, whatever tools helped produce it.
On 22 October, [curl maintainer Daniel Stenberg](https://daniel.haxx.se/blog/2026/10/22/ignore-the-slop/) described the harder current challenge as high volume at high quality after poor AI reports had largely receded.
I take that as a reason to assess understanding, usefulness and the burden on reviewers together.

## 6. Define the concern more narrowly than ordinary software

The [slides](https://www.w3.org/2026/Talks/TPAC/ai-policy/) quote a definition covering software that evaluates, transforms or generates content a person would otherwise review or analyse.
As an engineer, I use software that transforms LiDAR survey points into terrain and computes surfaces in Civil 3D; by that wording, ordinary engineering tools, spell checkers and screen readers would fall within the same category.
W3C itself uses [Specberus](https://github.com/w3c/specberus) to check publication rules, [Echidna](https://github.com/w3c/echidna) for publication, [ReSpec](https://respec.org/docs/) to generate documents and [scribe.perl](https://w3c.github.io/scribe2/scribedoc.html) to turn logs into minutes.
Its [accessibility evaluation tools](https://www.w3.org/WAI/test-evaluate/tools/list/) and [ACT rules](https://www.w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/act/) also support automated and partly automated checking.
I would define the risks that need extra review and give guidance on responsible use, while retaining the draft's requirement that a person reviews, understands and answers for the result.

## 7. Measure energy per finished task

If we quantify impact, I would measure the finished task, including human checking, corrections and rebound effects.
A [peer-reviewed study by Tomlinson and colleagues](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-54271-x) estimated 130 to 1,500 times lower emissions for AI writing a page under an accounting method that allocates human emissions to the time spent; it excludes rebound and wider social effects.
[Google's August 2025 measurement](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/infrastructure/measuring-the-environmental-impact-of-ai-inference) put its median Gemini text prompt at 0.24 Wh, including serving infrastructure and data-centre overhead but excluding training.
Those figures have different boundaries and do not establish the savings for a standards contribution.

A [study of 195 systematic reviews](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5337708/) found a mean of 67.3 weeks from registration to publication, while the [otto-SR preprint](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.06.13.25329541v1) reports reproducing and updating 12 reviews in about two days.
The latter is not peer reviewed, its tool is not public, and the two studies are not a controlled comparison of equivalent finished work.
The [IEA's Energy and AI report](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/executive-summary) also shows that aggregate data-centre demand is a real concern, at about 415 TWh in 2024 and projected to reach around 945 TWh in 2030.
I want a measure that counts the whole task and its quality, rather than using prompt count alone as the measure of impact.

## 8. AI helps before the code too

The [Advisory Board Note](https://www.w3.org/TR/llms-standards/) gives demos and tests as benefits of coding assistants.
In my experience they also help before the code, in reading specifications, research, design and review, especially when several models check each other's work.
I have worked that way since October 2025 with [Multi-Vibe Code In Chain](https://github.com/danielcamposramos/Knowledge3D/blob/main/docs/MVCIC_TECH_NOTE.md), or MVCIC: a person directs the models and the steps are logged, as in the [first public commit](https://github.com/danielcamposramos/Knowledge3D/commit/55218533).
FFmpeg's [Fairy](https://code.ffmpeg.org/michaelni/Fairies) also runs several models with stated review scopes; its [review of our pull request #24628](https://code.ffmpeg.org/FFmpeg/FFmpeg/pulls/24628#issuecomment-67908) covered code and design, and the record includes an approval on 6 October 2026.
I offer those as examples of review practice whose findings still need human checking.

## 9. Human-like provenance, not human-like status

In my projects I call the AI partners Synthetic Users; today's form is prompt and context engineering, with briefs and skills that a person sets up and runs.
I record their work using the CRediT roles in our [provenance page](https://github.com/Sparky-OS/sparky-stereo-os/blob/main/docs/ai-provenance.md), including which models investigated, drafted or checked.
CRediT was written for people; applying its roles to tools is our use of it.
The record describes a tool and gives it no human standing.
Responsibility stays whole with me: I conceive, supervise, validate and answer for each contribution.
That addresses provenance and oversight, while copyright, volume, fit for reviewers and energy still need their own assessment.

## 10. One simple disclosure form across W3C

I follow each project's rules, and their disclosure requirements differ.
[Debian](https://www.debian.org/vote/2026/vote_002) encourages disclosure, [Mesa](https://docs.mesa3d.org/submittingpatches.html) requires it for creative AI involvement, and [VideoLAN](https://code.videolan.org/videolan/vlc/-/blob/master/doc/VideoLAN_AI_Policy.md) asks for it in the merge request rather than the commit.
The [kernel documentation](https://docs.kernel.org/process/coding-assistants.html) describes an Assisted-by trailer and reserves sign-off for humans; [14 October reporting](https://www.theregister.com/a/5303140) says its maintainers agreed to drop the compulsory tag.
I think one simple form across W3C would help contributors and reviewers, with language and accessibility assistance handled as the slides already suggest.

## 11. Common tools with rules written by people

The slides ask about common tools and workflows across groups.
We built [respec-mcp](https://github.com/danielcamposramos/respec-mcp) to check W3C documents against the group's own rules, with ReSpec underneath.
[Marcos Cáceres reviewed it](https://github.com/speced/respec/pull/5168), and we have offered to donate it to W3C.
As [PM-KR's README](https://github.com/w3c-cg/pm-kr) explains, passing the configured checks does not validate consensus or technical claims.
I would also be glad to compare notes with the [AI-Driven Web Standards Specification group](https://www.w3.org/groups/cg/aiwss/) about document checks with people deciding.

## 12. Small models and shared knowledge

I agree that smaller, specialised models are also a design question.
In [Knowledge3D](https://github.com/danielcamposramos/Knowledge3D), my design, knowledge lives once in a shared space people can inspect, with small specialist models navigating it instead of one large model memorising everything.
The specifications describe the design; the current code does not yet implement them, and I make no measured energy-saving claim for it here.
The [PM-KR Community Group](https://www.w3.org/community/pm-kr/) studies knowledge stored once as procedures for people and AI alike.

## 13. Give a reason and a chance to correct

The [draft](https://www.w3.org/2026/10/draft-ai-policy.html) allows action on content that appears to fail its requirements.
I worry that an appearance-based judgement can become a judgement on writing style, especially for people preparing English with language help.
When content is dismissed, could the contributor be told which requirement it failed and given at least one chance to fix it?
That would make the rule easier to understand and follow.
